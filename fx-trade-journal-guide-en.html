How to Keep a Forex Trading Journal
What to Record, How to Review, and How to Stick With It

Published: October 8, 2026 Reading time: 8 min

This article explains how to record and review your forex trades. It is not investment advice or a recommendation to buy or sell anything. The methods and metrics described here do not guarantee profits or a higher win rate, and all investment decisions are your own responsibility.

Traders often say your own trade history deserves a look before the next chart does. Yet a trading journal is also one of the classic habits people know they should keep and quietly abandon after a couple of weeks. This guide covers what to write down, how to make the habit last, and how to read your entries so you can see the tendencies in your own trading.

Why a trading journal helps you review your trades

When a losing streak drags on, the cause is sometimes less about market analysis and more about repeating the same mistake: moving a stop loss, averaging down without a plan, or closing a trade early because the floating loss became uncomfortable. Habits like these are hard to notice from memory alone. That is not a statistic, just an example of the kind of pattern that becomes easier to spot once it is written down. A journal turns your trading into facts and numbers you can look back on, so your review is based on what actually happened rather than how it felt.

It is often clearest when you put the entry reasons for your winning and losing trades side by side. If your results lean one way for a particular combination of session, currency pair and setup, that lean only shows up once enough trades have been logged. What shows up differs from trader to trader.

7 things to record for every trade

Trying to record everything is the fastest way to stop recording anything. Start with these seven fields.

  • Currency pair and direction (BUY/SELL) — The basics. Over time this tells you which pairs you actually trade most and how they have gone for you.
  • Entry price, exit price and lot size — You need these to work out pips and profit or loss.
  • Profit/loss and pips — Calculating these by hand is the single biggest reason journaling feels like a chore. Use something that does the math for you.
  • Entry reason — Why you got in at that moment: “pullback buy”, “range breakout”, “moving average cross”. Turning these into tags makes them easy to group later.
  • Exit reason — “Took profit as planned”, “hit my stop”, “closed on emotion”. Exits that were not part of the plan are worth a closer look during your review.
  • State of mind — Were you calm when you entered, or rushed and frustrated? Writing it down lets you check later whether your results look different depending on how you felt.
  • Chart screenshot — Numbers alone will not bring back what the market looked like at the time. A screenshot makes your monthly review far more concrete.

3 habits that keep your journal going

1. Make logging a trade take seconds

The number one reason trading journals die is that filling them in is a hassle. Right after a losing trade, your motivation to write anything is at its lowest. If you can finish an entry in a few taps on the spot — a fixed form, tags you pick instead of type — the friction that makes people give up mostly disappears.

2. Write the most when you least want to

Logging the good trades and skipping the bad ones is a mistake many people make without realizing it. The entries you write after a loss, or when you were emotional, are the ones that tell you the most. Make it a rule: every closed trade gets logged, win or lose.

3. Put your review on the calendar

It is easy to feel that logging is enough and never look back at the entries. Decide in advance when you will review — Sunday evening for the week, the last day of the month for the monthly summary — so your records actually turn into a review.

How to review what you have recorded

Once you have a few weeks of entries, these are the numbers to start with.

  • Win rate — The share of your trades that closed in profit. On its own it does not tell you whether you made or lost money overall.
  • Total pips — Looking at results in pips lets you compare across currency pairs without the differences in lot size and pip value getting in the way.
  • Profit factor (PF) — Gross profit divided by gross loss. Above 1.0 means your winners added up to more than your losers for the period. A win rate in the 40s can sit alongside a PF above 1.0 when winners are larger than losers, which is why it helps to read the two together.
  • By session and day of week — How your trades went in the Tokyo, London and New York sessions. Some traders also find patterns tied to particular weekdays.
  • By entry-reason tag — Compare win rate and PF across your setups (pullbacks, breakouts and so on) to see which ones your record supports and which it does not.

Working all of this out by hand every week is not realistic. A tool that tallies it for you each month cuts the time a review takes to a fraction.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

  • Giving up after a few days — Usually a sign of too many fields. Stick to the seven above, or just three or four until the habit forms.
  • Writing numbers and never reviewing them — Put the review in your calendar, or make looking at last month's numbers part of the first day of each month.
  • Only logging the good trades — Losing trades are some of the most useful data you have. Stick to the rule: every closed trade gets logged.
  • Reviewing on gut feeling — Instead of “that month felt good”, look at win rate, PF and pips. The numbers keep your self-assessment honest.

FAQ

Should I keep my trading journal in a notebook or an app?
Either works if you actually keep it up. A notebook gives you complete freedom, but you have to work out win rate and pips by hand every time, and that extra effort is a common reason people stop. An app with a fixed set of fields and automatic totals takes less effort per trade, which makes it easier to stay consistent.
How many trades do I need to log before I can see patterns?
There is no single number. It depends heavily on your trading style and how often you trade. Rather than aiming for a target count, start by building a habit of reviewing your journal once a month.
What is profit factor (PF)?
Profit factor is your gross profit divided by your gross loss. A value above 1.0 means your winning trades added up to more than your losing trades over that period. Because a low win rate can still go with a PF above 1.0 when winners are larger than losers, it is most useful read alongside your win rate.
