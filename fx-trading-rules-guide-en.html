How to Write Forex Trading Rules
You Can Actually Check

Published: November 27, 2026 Reading time: 7 min

This article explains how to keep and review your own forex trade records. It is not investment advice or a recommendation to buy or sell anything. The methods described here are not a guarantee of profits or of a higher win rate, and all investment decisions are your own responsibility. All numbers in this article are fictional examples for illustration.

Many traders have a plan in their head but cannot say, trade by trade, whether they followed it. Rules only help if you can check them. This article covers how to write rules as short yes-or-no checks, the kinds of rules traders commonly use, how to record compliance on each trade, how to compare results when rules were followed or broken, and how often to revise them.

What makes a rule checkable

A rule is checkable when, after the trade, you can answer it with a plain yes or no. Compare:

  • "Trade with discipline." — cannot be checked.
  • "Set the stop loss before entering." — yes or no.
  • "Don't risk too much." — cannot be checked.
  • "Risk no more than a fixed share of my balance per trade (my number: ___ %)." — yes or no, once you fill in your number.

Keep each rule to one sentence and one condition. If a rule needs "and" or "unless", split it in two. Five to eight rules is a practical size; with twenty, you will stop checking them.

Common kinds of rules

Traders' rules usually fall into a few groups. The examples below show the form a rule can take; they are not recommendations, and the numbers are yours to choose.

  • Before entry — "My setup is present on the higher timeframe." "No new position within ___ minutes of a scheduled major economic release."
  • Risk — "Stop loss set before entry." "Position size calculated from the stop distance, not guessed."
  • During the trade — "Stop loss is not moved further away." "No adding to a losing position."
  • Per day or session — "No more than ___ trades per day." "Stop for the day after ___ losses in a row."
  • State of mind — "No trade when I am tired or trying to win back a loss."

Position size from a stop distance can be worked out with a lot size calculator.

Recording whether you followed them

Check the rules on every trade, not just the losing ones. The simplest method is a checklist attached to each entry in your journal: tick each rule you followed. Two habits make this reliable:

  1. Read the rules before the trade. A quick look at your list before you open a chart catches many breaks before they happen.
  2. Tick them right after closing. Waiting until the weekend means you will remember the good trades more kindly than the bad ones.

Over time this gives you a simple number per period: the share of trades on which you followed every rule.

Comparing results with and without rules

Once you have a few weeks of ticks, split your trades into "all rules followed" and "at least one rule broken" and compare them. A fictional example from one month of 30 trades:

All rules followedA rule broken
Trades228
Wins102
Total+64 pips−71 pips

In this example the month's total of −7 pips hides two different stories: trades that followed the plan were positive, and a few rule-breaking trades took more than all of that back. Your own split may look nothing like this; the point is that you cannot see either story without recording compliance per trade. With small counts like these, treat the comparison as a prompt for questions, not as proof.

It is also worth checking which single rule is broken most often. That is usually the best candidate for next week's one change (see "How to Review Your Forex Trades Each Week").

When to revise your rules

Revise on a fixed schedule, such as once a month, rather than right after a loss. When you do:

  • Remove rules you never break and that never matter; they add noise to the checklist.
  • Rewrite rules you break often because they are unclear, not because you ignore them.
  • Add a rule only when your record shows a repeated problem it would have prevented.

Keep the old list with a date. Comparing periods is only meaningful if you know which rules applied when.

Common pitfalls

Rules that describe outcomes

"Only take winning trades" or "make ___ pips a day" are goals about results, which you do not control. Rules should be about actions you take before and during a trade.

Checking only the losing trades

If you only look for broken rules after a loss, you never learn whether rule-breaking winners were luck. Tick every trade.

Changing several rules at once

If you change four rules in the same week, you cannot tell which change made the difference. Change one at a time.

FAQ

How many trading rules should I have?
Few enough that you will check every one on every trade. Many traders find five to eight short rules workable; with a long list, checking becomes a chore and gets skipped.
Should I write rules about how much I want to make?
It is better to keep those as separate goals. Rules work best when they describe actions you control before and during a trade, such as setting a stop loss or limiting trades per day, rather than results.
What should I do when I keep breaking the same rule?
First check whether the rule is clear enough to follow at the moment of trading; rewrite it if not. Then make keeping that one rule your single change for the coming week, and see whether reading the rules before trading helps.
